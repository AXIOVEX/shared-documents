# AI exposure: task redesign across professions

## Separate capability, adoption and employment

BLS's AI exposure categories do not estimate job-loss probabilities or extinction dates. Occupational projections incorporate broader demand and workforce dynamics; they do not isolate an AI causal effect. The task examples below are planning hypotheses to validate with work samples and employers. Official projections provide demand context, not proof that a particular AI system can safely perform the task. [S09, S10]

| Field | Tasks to examine for automation or assistance | Responsibilities to preserve and strengthen | Curriculum / incumbent adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Software | Boilerplate, routine code changes, initial tests and documentation | Requirements, architecture, security, integration and acceptance decisions | Specification-driven delivery, independent tests, code review and operational ownership |
| Accounting | Data entry, classification, reconciliation drafts and routine reports | Controls, exceptions, audit evidence, tax/regulatory interpretation and client decisions | ERP/data stewardship, analytics and reviewed automation |
| Legal support | Document summaries, first-pass discovery and drafting | Source accuracy, privilege, evidence custody, case context and attorney accountability | Citation checking, secure search and supervised review |
| Engineering design | Drawing production, candidate design generation, simulation setup | Physical constraints, tolerances, safety, manufacturability and validation | CAD/CAM plus metrology, simulation verification and physical testing |
| Administration / customer service | Routing, scheduling, standard forms and first responses | Escalation, complex resolution, data stewardship and relationship management | Process mapping, exception handling, accessibility and service quality measurement |
| Media / design | Draft copy, routine graphics, layout variants and transcription | Original investigation, creative direction, audience understanding and rights | Evidence/provenance, user research, editorial review and domain expertise |
| Manufacturing | Visual screening, planning suggestions and maintenance alerts | Machine safety, root-cause diagnosis, process ownership and physical execution | Sensors, controls, quality systems, reliability and supervised AI inspection |
| Medical imaging | Worklist prioritization, measurements, segmentation and report assistance | Clinical interpretation, patient context, procedures and accountable escalation | Role-specific AI validation, imaging informatics and monitoring |

**Confidence:** task redesign is a practical planning direction; exact employment effects and timing are uncertain. Verify actual deployment, cost, failure rate, regulation, liability and customer acceptance. An AI benchmark is not a staffing plan. Faster work can reduce labor per case, increase service volume, or both; total staffing depends on demand and implementation choices.

## Protect the route into experienced work

If routine junior tasks disappear, employers can also lose the training pathway that produces experienced staff. Universities and firms should deliberately supply supervised practice, realistic failure cases and increasing responsibility. Require students to explain their reasoning, validate tools against a baseline and complete some foundational work without automation. Then assess whether using AI improves quality and completion time without concealing errors.

For employers, measure novice hiring, mentoring hours, task reassignment and promotion separately from aggregate productivity. Do not interpret higher output per worker as proof that layoffs are needed, or proof that nobody will be displaced. Preserve a review route for workers whose tasks are automated.

## Medical imaging case study

Radiology is one example among several exposed fields, not a proxy for all medicine. Official national and Michigan projections do not identify physician radiologists as a disappearing occupation. ACR's 2026 workforce review points to continuing shortages. Physician radiologists, radiologic technologists, medical physicists and imaging-informatics staff have different training and responsibilities. [S09, S12, S14]

The joint AAPM/ACR/RSNA/SIIM education framework distinguishes clinical users, purchasers, clinical collaborators and developers. Use those roles to choose depth of training. Not every radiologist needs to become a model developer. [S15]

**Proposed shared core:** dataset quality; sensitivity/specificity and predictive values; prevalence and subgroup effects; external validation; image acquisition differences; uncertainty; automation bias; model drift; privacy/security; and escalation when a system fails. Clinical users need competent interpretation of outputs. Purchasers need evidence appraisal and workflow evaluation. Clinical collaborators need protocol design and annotation/validation literacy. Developers need engineering depth, reproducibility and lifecycle monitoring. This curriculum proposal is informed by the multisociety framework and FDA lifecycle material; it is not an accredited syllabus. [S15, S16]

**Proposed practical assessment:** compare a tool with a reviewed reference on appropriately governed cases, examine failure subgroups, document a deployment boundary, and define stop/escalation criteria. Use supervision appropriate to each profession. Patient data access and clinical deployment require their own governance.

Cross-modality learning can help clinicians contribute more broadly, but AI training alone does not qualify a professional to interpret every medical modality or practice another specialty. Required education, privileges and competence still apply. Track diagnostic quality, turnaround, access, workload and staffing together; no promise of job preservation follows from course completion.
